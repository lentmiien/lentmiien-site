We have reached Challenger Deep, near the southern end of the Mariana Trench. A modern pressure-based survey estimated its deepest observed seafloor at ten thousand, nine hundred and thirty-five metres, plus or minus six. That is nearly eleven kilometres of water overhead. Pressure here is about eleven hundred times the pressure at the surface. No fish has been observed at this depth, yet the bottom is not empty. Small amphipods hurry across the silt and gather wherever a rare meal appears. Pressure-loving microbes work inside the mud, too small for us to see. Deep animals usually avoid fragile, gas-filled spaces. Their watery bodies press inward and outward together, and their cells use special molecules to keep important proteins in shape. Look at the curved little amphipod near our light. Its relatives live in many waters, including the beach hoppers beneath seaweed at the very start of our journey. From the strand line to the deepest known seafloor, one crustacean family has found many ways to belong. The connection is small enough to hold in a hand, and large enough to cross the whole ocean.
