We pass one thousand metres, and the last sunlight is gone. This is the midnight zone: cold, wide, and black. Darkness does not make every eye useless. A giant squid has eyes up to about thirty centimetres across, large enough to gather very faint clues. It has eight arms for holding and two longer feeding tentacles that can shoot forward toward prey. Far away, a sperm whale closes its own eyes and sends a powerful click. The sound crosses the water, touches an object, and returns as an echo. From that returning sound, the whale can hunt in darkness. Below the squid, a much smaller anglerfish carries another tool. Its lure glows through bioluminescence: light made by a chemical reaction in a living thing. None of these animals turns night into day. They work with whispers of information—a glow, an echo, a silhouette, a vibration. The deep sea rewards patience and careful senses. Even here, animals must find food, avoid danger, and somehow locate another of their own kind in an enormous world.
