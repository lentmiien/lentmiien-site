Now the seafloor rises into view. Cold seawater slips through cracks in the crust, warms beside hot rock, and returns carrying dissolved minerals. When that water meets the cold ocean, minerals form a chimney and a dark plume. It looks like smoke, but there is no fire here, and no river of lava. This is a hydrothermal vent. Around it stands a crowded garden of giant tube worms, mussels, limpets, shrimp, and crabs. How can a feast begin where sunlight never arrives? The first cooks are microbes. They take energy from chemicals such as hydrogen sulfide and use it to make food, a process called chemosynthesis. Some giant tube worms have no mouth, gut, or anus. Instead, billions of helpful bacteria live inside them and prepare their nutrition. The worm’s red plume gathers oxygen and chemicals from the water for this partnership. When scientists first saw thriving vent communities in nineteen seventy-seven, the discovery changed ideas about where a food web could begin. Sunlight powers most familiar life, but chemistry opens another door.
