At ten centimetres, scale becomes personal. A hand can cup water, test a surface, cradle a stone, or show another person how large something was. Bodies served as convenient measures long before standardized rulers, but they also reveal why standards matter: no two hands are exactly alike. Touch changes our sense of size. A ridge too small to see may still catch a fingertip; a grain becomes roughness; a warm cup fits a palm. Measurement is not only numbers read by machines. It began as a conversation between bodies and the world.
