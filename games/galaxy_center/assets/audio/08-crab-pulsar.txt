A rhythmic signal reaches us from the wreckage of a star.

About six thousand five hundred light-years away, the Crab Nebula is the expanding remnant of a stellar explosion that people on Earth saw in the year ten fifty-four. At its center is not an ordinary star and not a black hole. It is a neutron star: the crushed core left behind when a massive star collapsed.

The Crab’s neutron star is only about ten kilometers across, small enough to fit inside a city, yet it holds more mass than our Sun. It spins about thirty times every second.

Beams of radiation sweep from its magnetic poles like lighthouse beams. The star does not truly blink on and off. We detect a pulse whenever a beam points toward Earth, which is why this kind of neutron star is called a pulsar.

The pulsar also pumps energy into a ring of material and two jets. X-ray, visible-light, and radio telescopes reveal different parts of that restless structure. Our scene combines those invisible and visible clues.

Remember this ring: it belongs to an energized neutron star. The very different dark object at our destination will need a different set of clues.
