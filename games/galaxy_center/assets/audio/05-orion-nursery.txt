Planets need stars, and stars begin in places like this: the Orion Nebula, about fifteen hundred light-years from Earth.

At first, a stellar nursery is a cold cloud of gas and dust. Gravity slowly pulls denser pockets inward. A pocket squeezes, warms, and begins to spin. When its center becomes hot and dense enough, a new star switches on. Leftover material flattens into a disk around it, where grains can bump, stick, and perhaps grow into planets.

Astronomers call some of these young disks proplyds, short for protoplanetary disks. Hubble has resolved proplyds around nearly two hundred stars in Orion.

Four massive young stars called the Trapezium blaze near the nebula’s bright center. Their fierce ultraviolet light makes gas glow, carves cavities, and can even blow material away from nearby disks. A nursery is beautiful, but it is not quiet.

The colors here combine clues from several kinds of light, and the disks are enlarged enough for us to notice them. Still, the central idea is real and wonderfully familiar: our Solar System also began with a young Sun wrapped in a dusty, planet-making disk.
