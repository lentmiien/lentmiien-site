Now our imagined route turns inward across the Milky Way’s disk.

Spiral arms are not solid ropes of stars. They are busy regions where gas and dust gather more densely. Clouds collapse, young stars switch on, and the most massive newborn stars shine blue. Between the arms, older and quieter stars continue their own orbits. Farther inward, stars thicken into an elongated central bar and a bright bulge.

Mapping all of this from Earth is like mapping a forest while standing among its trees. Dust hides distant regions, and every star is moving.

The European Space Agency’s Gaia mission helped by measuring positions, colors, and motions again and again. From twenty fourteen to twenty twenty-five, Gaia made more than three trillion observations of two billion stars and other objects. Enormous as that sounds, two billion is only about one percent of the stars thought to live in the Milky Way.

Our Sun travels with the disk too. One orbit around the galactic center takes roughly two hundred and thirty million Earth years. Astronomers call that a galactic year.

The map is magnificent, incomplete, and still changing as new measurements arrive.
