We have reached the center of the Milky Way.

Sagittarius A star is a supermassive black hole about twenty-seven thousand light-years from Earth. It packs roughly four point three million times the Sun’s mass into an astonishingly compact region.

A black hole is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner. Faraway stars can orbit it safely, just as planets orbit the Sun. But close to it, gravity shapes space and time dramatically. Beyond the event horizon, not even light can return.

How can we know something dark is there? For decades, astronomers followed stars whipping around an unseen point. One, called S2, completes an orbit in about sixteen years. Near the black hole it travels at almost three percent of the speed of light, and its path bends in the way Einstein’s theory predicts.

Then eight radio observatories across Earth worked together as the Event Horizon Telescope. Data gathered in twenty seventeen produced the image released in twenty twenty-two: a dark shadow surrounded by glowing gas and radio light bent by gravity.

Our scene combines that radio ring with an imagined time-trail of an orbiting star. It is not a naked-eye view. It is a portrait made from evidence—the quiet ending point of our journey and one of astronomy’s greatest team efforts.
