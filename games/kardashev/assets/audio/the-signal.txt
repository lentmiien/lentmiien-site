In 1964, radio astronomer Nikolai Kardashev was not designing a video-game technology tree. He was asking what kinds of distant civilizations our telescopes might actually notice. A society with more power could drive a stronger transmitter, and large engineering would leave larger traces. So he sketched three energy scales: one comparable to a world, one to a star, and one to a galaxy. The familiar thresholds in this journey—ten to the sixteenth, ten to the twenty-sixth, and ten to the thirty-sixth watts—come from a later logarithmic normalization often associated with Carl Sagan. That distinction matters. The scale is a measuring stick, not a prophecy, and definitely not a score for wisdom. Its enduring trick is simple: technology must obey physics. If it moves energy, rearranges matter, or dumps heat, the universe may keep the receipt. Tonight, we follow that receipt outward.
